IRON ORE RESOURCES OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA.

  In any discussion of the iron ore resources of South Australia it has
always to be borne in mind that, in a country of great distances, with in
many cases complete absence of transport facilities, bodies of iron ore are
valueless when the cost of transport becomes important. Consequently,
outlying or inaccessible deposits are not always recorded by the discoverer,
and as settlement and transport facilities increase, so will the number of
localities in which iron deposits are known to be augmented.
  The discovery of the mines of Broken Hill and the erection of lead
smelters at Broken Hill, Port Pirie, Dry Creek, and Port Adelaide, caused
a demand for ironstone flux which was met by the exploitation of many
deposits throughout the country.         Size was of less consequence than
reasonable purity and ease of transport, as the raiser found a ready market
for even limited quantities. The effect of the closing down of the scattered
smelters and the concentration of effort at Port Pirie led to the abandon-
ment of many of the flux workings, and when the Iron Knob was connected
by a tramway to the port of Hummocky Hill, now known as Whyalla, in
1903, the ease of slipplying very high-gra~e hrematite and its low cost caused
the cessation of work in all .the other ironstone quarries. How widespread
was the call for flux, whether for lead or copper smelting, may be gathered
from the following list in which all iron ore localities known to the Depart-
ment of Mines are recorded. Those that are known to have supplied flux
are .italicised, while those that are described later are shown by an asterisk.
   Angaston*, Barossa'», Beltana, Billeroo*, BtlI'm, Bi1'dwood, Clinton"",

   While the majority of these deposits yielded or could yield flux, many
are, by reason of their size, grade, and presence of deleterious constituents,
unsuited for the manufacture of iron. Others a'gain are ruled out by
reason of their inaccessibility, a term which must be taken to include the
expense of bringing the ore to fuel-that is to the seaboard, or fuel from the
seaboard to the ore.
                                      USES.
  The uses to which deposits may be turned are :-(a) Steel making,
(b) foundry pig, (c) flux, (d) ochres, and (e) road material; and the
requirements for each class are briefly set out below.
   ~(z) Steel Making.-The high cost of blast furnace plant and mill and the
great capacity of a modern blast furnace are such that it is considered that
a working,life of not less than 25 years must be counted upon as a minimum
before any deposit can receive serious consideration for this purpose.
Modern blast furnaces produce' as much as 180,000 tons of pig annually,
                                            8

and the average output of pig iron per furnace in 1916, based on the total
production of pig iron and the number (448) of blast furnaces in the
United States*, was 88,000 tons.
   This average rate of production would call for about 150,000 tons of
60 per cent. ore annually, equivalent to a deposit of 3,750,000 tons, on a 25
years' life for the plant. Examination of the records of a large number
of plants shows that they control much larger reserves than are indicated
by this calculation. It may be noted that the Broken Hill Proprietary
Company's steel works, operating three blast furnaces, are capable of pro-
ducing about 450,000 tons of pig annually, and that the cost of the wholc
plant, which, however, is designed with a view to the erection of further
blast furnaces, was £5,039,107 (exclusive of the depreciation written off) 011
May 31st, 1921.
   From these figures it may be seen that the ore body justifying the
erection of a modern steel-making plant must be a large one.
   In addition to tonnage, other conditions must be favorable before a
deposit can be considered suitable for steel work, viz. :-Relation to fuel.
flux, and market for the product, and whether the site for the proposed
works is such that the cost of assembling coal, flux, and ore, and of placing
the product on the market, is the minimum possible. The Broken Hill
Proprietary, for example, working the largest known iron ore body in South
Australia-and probably in Australia-decided that Newcastle, New South
Wales, best fulfilled these conditions, and established their works there.
near the centres of coal production and greatest metal consumption.
   Of very great commercial importance is the nature of the impurities in
the iron ore. Silica, alumina, lime, magnesia, and others of less importan:ce
can he removed in the slag at additional costi for flux and fuel, but do not
affect the quality of iron produced. On the other hand, certain elements.
though present in small amount, enter the iron almost wholly, and affect it
very definitely. Phosphorus, sulphur, titanium, and manganese may be
cited, although under certain conditions manganiferous pig is intentionally
 produced. Titanium is regarded as undesirable in excess of 1 per cent. in
the ore, as its elimin:ation in the furnace requires more fuel and reduces the
rate of smelting. Phosphorus is regarded as very deleterious, unless
 present in large amount, when it can be turned to account by the manu-
facture of basic slag fertiliser as a by-product. The percentage present in
 the pig iron: determines the process that has to be adapted in the manufac-
 ture of steel.
   The following table from Eckel (Iron Ores, p. 151) shows the allowable
 phosphorus in pig iron for various utilisations:-
      Acid open hearth-Less than .05 per cent.
      Acid Bessemer-Less than .1 per cent.
      Basic open hearth for normal process-Not over 1.5 per cent. and.
           preferably, not over 1 per cent.; for special processes, 1.5 per cent.
           and over.
      Basic Bessemer-At least 1.5 per cent., preferably over 2 per cent.
      Foundry Iron-Wide range, according to special use of iron.
    From this summary it may be seen that the requirements for steel making
 are strict, and include size of deposit, relation of deposit to fuel and
 market, and chemical composition of ore.

 market pig iron only, the capital cost is much reduced, the amount to be set

     Owing to the comparatively high value of ochre and the relatively small

     (e) Road Material.-Where deposits of limonitic iron ores are availablE'

                             VAL DE OF IRON ORE.
     A good deal of misconception appears to be present locally as to the
  monetary value of ores of iron. Iron ore prices are ultimately based upon
  eit.her demand or cost of production. As the iron smelting industry of
  Australia is at present constituted, there are two works in New South Wales
  and one projected in Queensland. Of the New South Wales plants, one at
                                      10
Lithgow, in the Blue Mountains, depends on ores raised in New South
Wales; the other at Newcastle draws its supplies from the Iron Knob, in
South Australia.
   There is a blast furnace at Mittagong, in New South Wales; but it has
not been operated for many years, and only produced pig iron.
   The site of the Queensland plant has been announced as at Bowen, and
press reports have stated that ore will be drawn from Mount Biggenden
(near Maryborough) and Mount Leviathan (at Cloncurry).         The latter
deposit is large, and of good quality, but is 481 miles by rail from sea-
board. 'l'he Queensland Government has also secured control of very largl'
and high-grade ironstone deposits at Yampi Sound, on the north-west coast
of Western Australia, from which it is intended to draw supplies for the
smelters.
    It is thus obvious that the demand for South Australian ore, other than
that of Iron Knob, is not likely to be serious, unless the cost f.o.b.
approximates the cost of mining and placing on ship of Iron Knob ore or of
the ores used in other plants. So long as the Newcastle Works have their own
a1l1ple deposits to draw upon they cannot be expected to give a price above
that at which they can raise ore themselves, and this price is below what may
be called the commercial valuation of the ore. Such commercial valuation
only obtains when there is competition between numerous smelters for a
reasonable proportion of the ore that they require. This is exemplified
in the case of two markets, called by Eckel the "Lake Superior Market,"
which for 1903-1913 averaged 7 cents (3-!d.) per unit of iron, and the
"Atlantic Market." In the "Atlantic Market," which includes all ore
sold at the consuming ports of the North Atlantic, prices ranged from
5-?t rents (2!d.) to 8-2- cents (4id.) per unit of iron, depending on quality
These rates show an average value for 60 per cent. ore of approximately 20s.
per ton, but the price in the last few years has been considerably higher. It
is thus apparent that, even assuming a "buying" market to exist, the price
of, say, 60 per cent. South Australian iron ores cannot be such that, under
modern economic conditions, an export trade beyond Australia can be
established.

                           History of Development.
   The main lode has long hecn known, and has been worked fr(,Jn time
to time as a source of iron flux. The mining for flux has been unsystematic,
and a considerable number of excavations have been made at various
points along the outcrop. From the largest of these openings, usually
referred to as the "Big Face," some 12,000 tons of ore have been removed
as far as may be judged from the dimensions of the excavation.       It does
not appear probable that more than 40,000 tons of iron ore hav'e boon
removed from the lode over its entire length.
   Some experimental smelting of the ore was attempted many years ago,
and pig iron is reported to have been actually produced at Gawler. But
the quantity tested was small, and no further attempts were made to
utilise the ore as a source of iron.    This being so, it is not a matter for
surprise that no effort has been made to pierce the lode across its width
at a number of places in order to provide accurate data for the estimation
of quantities.    The amount of work to be done to give this information
is not large, as short tunnel crosscuts could be inexpensively driven on the
levels of existing benches.      The workings now open for observation are
superficial and are located in all cases upon the hanging-wall side of the
principal lode, since that portion has been most accessible, and the open
cast working has hitherto involved very little stripping of overburden.

                                     Geology.
   The ore body which constitutes the main lode has certainly been formed
by the more or less complete replacement of a series of tilted sedimentary
rocks, with the dip of which it conforms in a general way.
   The iron-bearing material is hrematite in all cases, save where hydration
at the surface has resulted in the formation of an insignificant amount of
limonite.    The variety of hrematite represented is that known as
specularite, and in most of the excavations the micaceous form of this
variety is abundantly represented.
   Intimately associated with the hrematite are present quartz pebbles and
some argillaceous material, both of which represent the undigested residues
of the beds of sediment which have suffered replacement. In the majority
of the exposures the undigested matter is disseminated through the iron
ore; but in the tunnel on the bank of Victoria Creek, at the southern
boundary of mineral lease 2081 (now M.L. 2428), there is a well-defined
inclusion of country rock, i.e., a "horse," the contents of which are to be
discarded in mining.
   Small veinlets of quartz extend outwards from the iron lode into thc
country rock on the hanging-wall side.     'l'hese veinlets are approximately
perpendicular to the plane of the lode.
   The principal lode extends north for a distance of 29 chains from the
southern extremity of the leases. To the north of this lode, and 9i chains
distant from its northern extremity, there lies another lode of similar
material which may be traced on the surface for 7 chains. 'fhere is a
still more northerly outcrop of low-grade iron ore on the western bounda.ry
of mineral lease 2079 (now :M.L. 2430).
   These three lodes appear to be parts of one lode system, the components
of which are disposed en echelon.                   .

  'rhe former of the two last-mentioned outcrops has been worked to a small

   '1'he main lode extends beyond the limits of the area held under mineral
lease, and was traced south for a distance of 30 chains from the South
Para River.      Beyond this point the outcrop was not followed.      Very
little work has been done upon this south extension of the lode, since the
outcrop does not rise to such heights above the river level as it does
within the mineral leases to the north, and the tonnage to be obtained
from open cuts is correspondingly smaller.
   The width of the lode cannot be exactly determined for lack of the
necessary exposures. The outcrop varies very considerably in width on
account of the comparatively low angle of dip and the relation of the ore
body to the topography.     Where the overburden of sedimentary rocks
has not been removed by denudation the outcrop is narrow, but where
extensive natural stripping of the overburden has occurred the outcrop
is broad.
  Existing exposures show the lode to have a width of at least 16ft., and
that at some points this width is exceeded. For the purpose of calculation
of tonnage it appears that the width of 16ft., measured at right angles to
the dip-plane, may be reasonably taken as the average smallest dimension
of the lode. It may be that future prospecting operations will extend this
estimated average width with the possible addition of another 25 per cent.
  The strike of the principal lode is, when the whole length is taken into
consideration, 10° west of north, and that of the larger of the two ore
bodies in minerai lease 2079 (now M.L. 2430) is 38 ° we-st of north. The
dip is in each case to the ea,st, and the angle of dip varies from point to
point between 40° and 50°.
   There has apparently been a small amount of displacement of the lode
at a period subsequent to its formation, but only portions of the ore body
are affected, and the general continuity of the lode is beyond doubt. There
is apparently a fault plane at the north end of the Big Face, which
truncates the hanging-wall side of the lode. Its strike is 10° east of north,
and its dip 76° towards the cast.      'rhe exposure of this fault is only a
small one, but it does not appear to affect the continuity of the lode either
to the north or to the south. It is possible that this fault is related to the
lode channel, and that it actually antedates the period of ore formation,
but further exposures are necessary before the matter can be definitely
proved.    A small overthrust fault, marked by the displacement of the
quartz veilliets by a few inches, is visible in the hanging-wall country at
this place. On the south bank of the South Para River there is a duplica-
tion of the outcrop of similar lode matter which appears to be due to a
normal strike fault with a westerly dip.

                                  Ore Type!.
    The predominant ore in both the Iron Monarch and the Iron Knob
deposits is hrematite, which, when pure, contains 70 per cent. of metallic
 iron. For the most part it is massive and dense, and in places shows
schistose structure. Some is earthy and pulverulent, and some, containing
 a proportion of manganese dioxide, is blue-black and exceedingly dense.
    In the Iron Monarch magnetite is present. The writer was informed
 that near the summit the compass is deflected by magnetite. This also
 occurs on portions of the north-western slope, and a low-lying outcrop to
 the north-west is highly magnetic.
    Associated with these, the primary and important ores, are occurrences
 of secondary and superficial limonite.
    It occurs in two main type localities, and the deductions to be drawn
 from its presence are not necessarily identical.' Where erosion is active,
 as on the slopes of the hill, the hrematite is visible to the surface, but on
 the plateau-like crest, where drainage is comparatively inactive and soil
 is present, there is formed a crust of nodular and massive limonite.
    On some of the slopes where schist is present beneath the soil there is a
marked segregation of limonite into a superficial crust. This is especially
notable on the road cut in the hillside on mineral lease 1662. In places
the limonite may be seen penetrating the jointing and crevices of the schist
for a few inches, but on the slopes it is essentially superficial in habit.
On the plateau, however, though there are doubtless bands of schist
 concealed beneath the soil and limonite, conditions of drainage and
retention of iron-laden moisture by the soil are such that limonite is
abundantly formed, and may mask not only the schist bands, but the
hrematite itself. The costeans on this plateau, although yielding high-grade
limonite, have not been deep enough to definitely prove whether the
 limonite is simply a widespread sheet irrespective of the underlying
material, or whether, as in the cases of the Iron Baron and the western
slope of miner·al lease 1662, it indicates the presence of schist. Deeper
costeaning from north-east to south-west is advisable to clear up this point,
which has a bearing on the future extension of the quarries.
   Many samples were formerly tak.en from these shallow costeans, in
which all the ore exposed is essentially limonite, and it is probable that the
results are lower than if the trenches had reached the hrematite.
   Two accessory minerals are present in the soil and superficial portions
of the deposits-gypsum -and carbonate of lime. Inappreciable in amount,
they are of interest by reason of their occurrence.
   The gypsum forms as small crystals and aggregates of crystals in the
soil and crevices, while the carbonate of lime forms a travertinous coating
on the ironstone blocks. Some, seen on the very peak of the Iron Knob
prior to its removal, had the appearance of masses of travertine until
lifted or broken.
   In Bulletin No. 8, G,S'~'.A., page 89, the writer pointed out that the
gypsum associated with cyclic salt was considerable in amount, and, with
the climatic conditions in the more arid portions of the State, was to a
large extent retained in the soil. The same source may be ascribed in
part to the carbonate of lime, 'which is found in the soil as a travertine
layer, and in the iron ore in the joints and cleavages from just below the
surface to a depth of 20ft. to 25ft.

                                  Manganese.
   The Iron Knob ore body is very free from manganese, hut portions of
the Iron Monarch deposit are highly manganiferous. The maximum
development is in the vicinity of B Quarry and the spur to the eastward.
Up to 17 per cent. of metallic manganese is recorded in some places, though
the average of 'B Quarry is considerably lower.       On the outcrop above
B Quarry there are considerable areas in which the manganese exceeds
1 per cent., and these areas are shown on the accompanying plan. They
are based on samples of ore from outcrops or shallow trenches.
   The highest grade manganese ore in lumps is shipped to Newcastle and
used in the open hearth furnaces, but a considerable tonnage of high-
manganese fines is accumulating on a dump.
   The low-manganese ore is blended with the non-manganiferous hrematite
to bring the amount to such a percentage as will not affect the working
of the furnaces or raise the manganese tenor of the ore above 1 per cent.
   Manganese being deposited somewhat less readily than iron, it is
reasonable to expect that the upper portion of the deposit contains more
than the average. At the same time, B Quarry in places is highly
manganiferous to floor level, and the manganese extends down the eastern
spur below floor level.
   The deepest portion of A Quarry at a lower horizon and the shaft below
the floor of B Quarry, 'are practically non-manganiferous, and it appears
probable that boring will show that the ultimate solution of the difficulty
due to manganese will be the opening of qnarries lower down the slopes,
the production of which, blended with the manganiferous ore, will redUCE
the proportion to the desired limit.
   Although there is a considerable quantity of manganiferous ore on the
east side, the size of the deposit is very great, and the ore on the western
slope of the hill is not noticeably m·anganiferous, so that the extension of
A Quarry in this direction will regUlate the grade from the upper benches
now being worked. At present the disposition of the manganese on th(:
outcrop of the deposit is a serious disability in the quarrying of the ore.
                             The Waste Zones.
   The detailed examination that it was possible to make with the aid of
the subdivislonal lines marked out on the ground by the Company, led to
the recognition of several zones of schist, more or less ferruginous, in
the ore body. It was also possible to differentiate ore outcropping in
place, in which alone these waste bands could be traced, and the limonite·
covered area of the plateau. Vertical sections of A Quarry also showed
waste zones in the face.
  Faces advancing north-easterly, parallel to the trend of the waste zones,
are disadvantageously situated as a large zone may be encountered
simultaneously along the face. Further the dip to the north-east locks the
waste into the ore, rendering separation difficult. The best face of attack
would be a stepped face from the north-east coming south-west, but the
high manganese in the eastern portion of the deposit renders the opening
of such a face difficult lIDless B Quarry be extended along the course of
the dyke, past the manganiferous area into the solid hrematite of the north-
western section of the ore body. It appears that the carrying of the north-
west end or A Quarry round the west side of the hill, and then working
eastward, will reduce the inconvenience caused by these waste zones.

                                    Methods.
   The ore is worked open cast in three main quarries, and no overburden
is present. There are, however, certain bands of waste rock which are, as
far as possible, broken separately and placed in waste dumps.
   Drilling is done by hammer drills, using hollow steel, and driven by
compressed air from a central power-plant serving the three quarries.
   The broken materials are loaded by hand or by two steam shovels into
five-ton bottom-discharge trucks, which are lowered by gravity planes to the
storage bins beside the tramway.
   Prom these bins the ore is taken in 15-ton hopper trucks to Whyalla,
and after crushing, is dumped t.o await shipment.
   The storage dump, capable of containing 30,000 tons, of which half is
"-live" ore, extends over the first of a series of conveyor beIts, on to which
the ore is led by chutes, and taken along a jetty for a distance of 2,400ft.
to be discharged into vessels of 5,000 to 6,000 tons capacity.
   'l'he output at the present time is ·about 15,000 tons a week, and this will
be readily increased as the demand warrants.
   The township of Whyalla is the local headquarters of the company, and.
a large modern power-housc furnishes power for the crushing and trans-
porting of the ore, the lighting of the town, and '3. large fitting shop. In
addition there is plant for the distillation of sea water for engine and
domestic use.
   In conjunction with these works, numerous cottages have been erected
by the company for the housing of their employes at Whyalla and Iron
Knob. The older ones are of wood and iron, bllt the more recent are
built of reinforced concrete.       In both townships a boarding-house, or
"mess camp," has been built by the company for the convenience of t.he
single men. The latter are housed either in tents or in the series of
reinforced concrete quarters which are superseding the former.
   Two large and modern institute buildings, with large hall, reading,
writing, billiard and games rooms, have been rccently completed.
                                      Concltt.Sion.
  'l'he ore bodies of the Iron Knob and Iron Monarch are deep-seated in
their origin, and are likely to persist to ·a very considerable depth. Below
750ft. R.L. the ore is proved to extend another 210ft. in depth by one bore-
hole, and this at a point where the observed dip of the footwall or bottom
of the deposit indicated that bottom would be reached at about this depth.

To the north of the borehole the ore may be expected to persist to a
considerably greater depth. On calculating the tonnages to RL. 540ft. for
the main body, and to depths easily attainable in the lesser bodies, ore to
the extent of 133,000,000 tons can be safely estimated as in sight. To this
level there should be but little waste to be removed from the boundaries
of the ore bodies, but a proportion of waste, estimated at almost 10 per
cent. in bulk, additional to the iron ore tonnage, will have to be disposed of.

                                      Grade.
   The gangue minerals average less than 5 per cent., the balance of the ore
consisting of iron and manganese oxides.
   The manganiferous ore is somewhat irregularly distributed over the-
outcrop, and in places has a considerable vertical range (e.g., the height
of B Quarry). The ore highest in mang.1nese is useful for special purposes,
but the bulk requires to be blended to reduce the manganese content. '1'he
presence of these manganiferous areas to some extent determines the
position of the faces, and will cause difficulty in keeping the manganese
within the limit allowed, so long as the first "lift" is being worklOld. The
probability is that the lower portion of the deposit will be less mangani-
ferous. Should boring establish this, a face at a lower horir-on 'l'ould give
an opportunity to work in the excess manganese of the outcrop. '1' he
utilization of Middlebaek Range ore will do the same, as it is high grade
and free from manganese.
   In view of the large tonnage of nl:.1nganiferous ore and the difficulty of
keeping the manganese in the ore sent to Newcastle below oortain limits,
the question of its utilization is important.
   Some could probably be used as flux in the lead smelters at Port Pirie,
b\lt the making of a manganiferous pig iron will probably be the solution
of the difficulty. At present the high manganese p3.tches are so placed as
to make the direction of advance of the quarry faces liable to run into bars
of waste from the most awkward angle. When the first "lift" is taken off
the hill-which may not be for twenty years or more-it is unlikely that
there will be any excess of manganese. Indeed, it is probable that it should
be conserved by opening quarries lower down the hill to obtain manganese-
free iron to blend with the manganiferous ore.
   The value of these iron deposits is very great, and though mention has
been made of certain disabilities, the disabilities are, when compared with
those of other deposits in the world, trifling, especially when the very high
percentage of iron in the ore is taken into account. The deposits support
a considerable population in South Australia, and a still greater one in
Newcastle, where not only the steel works, but an ever-increasing group of
subsidiary industries essential to the wellbeing of Australia is established.
   The possession of two such exceptionally rich and large ore deposits '3S
those of the Iron Knob and Middlebaek ensures to South Australia for at
least a century a commanding place in the production of iron ore III
Australia.
   While it is to be regretted that conditions governed by the cost of
 assembling ore, coal, and flux, ·and the disposal of the product most
 economically, placed the steel works beyond the State's boundary, it must
 be recognised that it is better to supply raw material to be transformed into
 useful products as economically as possible, than to attempt to carryon an
 industry ·3.t a site where the natural conditions are less fav'orable, and
 where the cost of the product would be increased.

                                    Geology.
   'fhe hills containing the o]'e bodies are made up of rock of schistose or
slaty structure, very much altered by decomposition, and by siliceous
zones and bands or beds of intercalated hrematite and siliceous material.
'fhis latter rock will be referred to as ferruginous chert. The remainder
of the ridge consists of hrematite in veins and replacement masses. The
relationship of these rocks to one another is set out approximately on the
last plan.
   The similarity of these deposits in their geological aspects to the Iron
Knob and Iron Monarch deposits is very striking, and there is no room
for doubt but that they are identical in type, in time of formation, in the
character of the ores, and of the secondary rocks formed during the
deposition of the ore. The Iron Monarch replacement, however, has been
much more complete than in the case of the Middleback deposits, where
the country rock between the ore-filled fissures remains, and has not been
almost wholly replaced by hrematite. '1'he Middleback deposits, therefore,
have special interest in that they afford evidence as to the origin of both
deposits.
    'fhe oldest rocks of the district are slaty and schistose rocks, so much
decomposed wherever exposed that no very definite idea of their original
appearance can be formed.        They form the core of the Middleback: Range,
and enclose the iron-ore bodies, which, together with the ferruginous cherts,
have, by their resistance to degradation, formed the r,ange as a residual
block.      On either hand they are flanked by comparatively small develop-
ments of ferruginous chert, and som{1 of the ore bodies gradually grade into
this material as they are followed along their strike.
    'fhe gener'al strike is north by east to north-north-east, and there is a
transition along the line of strike from the argillaceous schist into quartzite
srhist. On either flank of the range the schist merges into gneiss.
    '1'he plains on either side of the range are covered by sand, which has
blown from the west through the gaps and covers the lowest saddle. The
gneiss extends for some distance to the east, to the Post-Terti,ary fault
forming the western limit of the coastal plain'"', and appears to have been
 penetrated by later, though still Pre-Cambrian granite, a typical example
 occurring at Carolby rockhole, in the Hundred of Ash.

 above the Tertiary coastal plain, consists of a tilted bed of coarse gritty
 quartzite, containing rounded stones of varying size irregularly dispersed
through it. These stones consist of quartz, white and dark quartzite,
 ferruginous chert, felspar and quartz porphyries, granite, and slate.

                           The Sow'ce of the Iron.
   Three sources are possible:-
     (a) From below by -:lscending solutions receiving their iron from
            relatively distant sources.
     (b) From above by descending solution, the iron being derived from
            material now denuded, such as-to instance the Iron Monarch
            -the ferruginous chert.
      (c) By lateral and descending segregation from the schists which now,
            in their decomposed state, contain a proportion of iron.
   In favor of the th90ry of -ascending solutions, is the fact that many of
the South Australian pegmatites contain hmmatite as an original mineral,
and the gneisses or pegmatised schists visible may well have been fels-
pathised from a deep-seated iron-bearing magma. The molecular replace-
ment of the schist and 0:1' breccias by ironstone showing the original
structure, is evidence of an active circulation of water and of considerable
chemical activity, and is such as is likely to have resulted from deep-seated
solutions.    Probably silica has been removed from the zone of ore
deposition and redeposited, along with a proportion of ironstone, to form
the cherts, but at a later stage these chert bodies have been fmctured with
the introduction of iron into the cracks.      Simultaneous origin is shown
by the merging of hmmatite into ferruginous chert.
   If descending solutions from material formerly overhead be considered,
there would be the objection that such large bodies as the Iron Monarch
would call for a very much larger bulk of the ferruginous chert to furnish
the iron than -appears likely to have existed, and under atmospheric
conditions this ferruginous chert is apparently very stable.
   In the case of the schists the oxidised material, when compared with the
few examples of unweathered schist exposed at Iron Monarch, appears
rather to have been iron-enriched than impoverished.
   On the whole it ~ppears most reasonable to assume that these bodies
have their origin in deep-seated solutions, and consequently may be
expected to have a considerable vertical range.
   The intensity of fracturing and the physical characteristics of the rock
at the time of fracturing, have had a pronounced influence on the ore
bodies. In the case of Middleback, the fracturing hail been along definite
lines, and the intervening rock has been insufficiently shattered to permit
the free circulation of the mineralising solution, and so replacement halil
been incomplete.
   At the Iron Monarch shattering has been mueh more intense, but there
-are certain well-defined zones, stFiking north-west and south-east and
dipping north-east, of a somewhat unctuous clay, which represent beds of
argillaceous rock that did not permit the formation of fractures in them,
or if such fractures fGrmed, they closed prior to mineralization, and so
these beds were not permeated and replaced by the iron-bearing solutions
to the same extent as the mass of the hill.
   Through the channels formed by this shattering, iron-bearing solutions
-probably alkaline carbonates-have made their way, dissolving the rock
adjacent to the fractures and replacing the schist molecularly, so that even
the highest grade ore may show the structure of the original schist. In
places the replacement is incomplete, shown either as incomplete replace-
ment of schist, or as a network of iron ore enclosing the untouched schist.
