1. INTRODUCTION

West of Lake Torrens, sediments of Upper Proterozoic
age were deposited on the tectonically stable Stuart Shelf.
. The Woocalla Dolomite lies disconformably on the
Pandurra Formation, and is overlain by the Whyalla Sandstone.
The unit crops out along the western shore of Pernatty Lagoon,
at Sweet Nell, at Woocalla quarry and at the northern edge of
Lake Dutton. In outcrop, the unit may be massive, laminated,
brecciated or stromatolitic (Johns, 1968). In the Woomera
Bore, the unit consists of 120 m of black shale. In the Winnie
Pinnie-Trevenna area, Noranda drill holes intersected grey
dolomitic shale and yellow to red clay (Appleyard, 1970).

With respect to base metal mineralisation, copper
occurs at the Sweet Nell Mine in a shale unit underlying
dolomite. High grade oxide copper ore was worked at this mine
in the early years of this century. In addition, Noranda inter-
sected minor Pb-Zn mineralisation (max. 0.63% Pb and 0.7% Zn) .
in dolomitic shale in the Winnie Pinnie-Trevenna area.

In the last few years, a large volume of data about
the Woocalla Dolomite has accumulated. The aim of this report
is threefold

oe

a) to summarise the available data;

b) to relate the observed facies changes to
the environment of deposition;

c) to draw attention to the possibilities of
economic base metal mineralisation in the
Woocalla Dolomite.

2. SUMMARY

Pre-Woocalla erosion of the Pandurra Formation
produced a surface of low ridges and stream channels leading
into broad valleys. The sediments of the Woocalla Dolomite
were deposited in a shallow sea on top of the post-Pandurra
palaeo-surface. Geological mapping and stratigraphic drilling
have shown that a variety of facies types can be recognised in
the Woocalla Dolomite, and these can be related to the environ-
ment of deposition as follows :-

Red and green shale
Dolomitic shale
Sandy dolomite

Laminated dolomite

Of these facies types, those deposited in deeper
water, in shallow lagoons and in the supratidal zone may be
associated with reducing conditions favourable for the
precipitation of sulphides.

Data from recent stratigraphic drilling and from the
Noranda exploration programme (carried out in 1968-71) indicate
that high copper values are restricted to two main facies of
the Woocalla Dolomite; the dolomitic mud and silt facies, and
the dolomitic shale facies. Within these facies types,
mineralisation appears to be related to local depressions in
the post-Pandurra palaeo-surface.

3. ‘FACIES VARIATIONS IN WOOCALLA DOLOMITE

It is apparent from geological mapping and drilling
that there are a number of significant facies changes within
the Woocalla Dolomite. Although there are large gaps in our
knowledge of facies types and distribution (due to lack of
outcrop and difficulty in identifying rock type in drill
cuttings), the following appear to be the main types. Numbers
in brackets refer to samples examined microscopically by
Mr. P.J. Curtis.

3.1 Black Shale (A9777)

This facies is a finely bedded black shale
consisting of clays, carbonate and silica with minor
graphite and sulphide. Graphite is considered to
represent original organic material. Details of sedi-
mentary structures are provided by Noranda drill logs.

In most places, black shale beds are separated
by thin grey dolomitic beds, usually less than 1 cm
thick and generally in the range 2-5 mm. The rock may
be laminated, with black mudstone laminae alternating
with grey carbonate laminae. In sections where there is
no dolomite, the rock is more massive. Beds may be
graded with black fissile mudstone at the base grading
up to shaley dolomite at the top. Current bedding is
recorded in several places but is not generally present.
Scattered chips and flakes of dolomite and occasional
thin sandy layers indicate minor intermittent influxes
of coarser detrital material into the basin of deposition.

Bedding is normally horizontal but is disturbed
here and there. by structures resulting from slumping ~— wavy
bedding, slumped bedding, pull-aparts, boudinage structures
and minor brecciation. The upper surfaces of slump
structures may be truncated to form micro-unconformitites.

A number of terms used in the Noranda drill logs
("disrupted bedding", "smashed dolomite layers", "pile-up
cascade structures", "diapiric structures") point to a
certain amount of plastic injection of clay through the
bedding prior to lithification.

Small scale faulting and one or two fault breccia
zones are reported in the Noranda logs. Much more common
are tension cracks, usually partly filled by calcite and
sulphides. Occasional small calcite-sulphide lenses,
apparently also formed by lateral secretion, are parallel
to bedding. Calcite crystals and sulphides are also
present in breccia zones.

Sulphides are visible in nearly all core inter-
sections and drill cuttings. Curtis (1973) reported that
the sulphide is mainly recrystallised framboids of pyrite
(Fig. 2) which is considered to have been precipitated by
organic processes. It occurs disseminated through the
shale and in fine bands (up to 1 mm thick) parallel to
bedding, as well as in lateral secretion veinlets. Other
sulphides are galena, sphalerite and chalcopyrite, the
last partly altered to chalcocite and covellite. They
occur as disseminated grains, as blebs along bedding
planes, as replacements of pyrite, and in lateral secretion
veinlets. The sphalerite is a pale honey colour, indicating
low iron content.

3.2 Dolomitic Shale

With increase in the proportion of carbonate
laminae to mudstone laminae, the black shale grades into
a dolomitic shale, which is usually grey to brown in colour,
with occasional thin bands of black shale. The rock is
laminated, and zones of slumping are reported in Noranda's
drill holes LD5 and LD6 (Thomas and Douch, 1971). As for
the black shale, this facies contains minor sulphide and
graphite.

3.3 Laminated Dolomite

In the Woocalla Quarry, the rock is a pink to
pale grey micritic dolomite, finely laminated, with
secondary manganese oxide along lamination planes and
in fractures. No sand grains, dolomitic mud clasts,
oolites or algal structures are visible. The dolomite
occurs in beds 10-200 mm thick, maintaining their thick-
ness and regular spacing for distances of 20-40 m. The
only bedding plane features are ripple marks with an
amplitude of about 10 mm and a wave length of around
50 mm.

The dolomite beds are separated by narrow partings
of white to pale yellow shale. Thickness of the shale is
generally only 1-2 mm, but increases towards the top of the
unit where shale beds of 20-50 mm thickness occur.

At irregular intervals the bedding is disturbed by
channel structures consisting of slumped dolomite beds,
shale lenses and some brecciated dolomite (see Fig. 3).

In the McLeay area, drill cuttings from holes OK20
and OK21 show that the Woocalla Dolomite here consists of
a very fine grained, tough dolomite, finely laminated with
white, green and pink bands. No sand grains, algal
fragments, oolites or clay lenticles are seen in hand
specimen. In this area, the laminated dolomite grades
upwards into dolomitic shale.

3.4 Sandy, Oolitic and Breccia Dolomite (A9882, A9883)

These facies occur in close association in the
field. Good outcrops are present along the western edge
of Pernatty Lagoon, but in drill cuttings it is impossible
to distinguish the various facies - the rock appears as
grey to white crystalline dolomite, sometimes massive,
sometimes bedded.

3.6 Dolomitic Mud and Silt

This facies of the Woocalla Dolomite outcrops
only in the vicinity of Sweet Nell (the so-called Sweet
Nell Shale) but has been cored (in holes MG14D and LyY3,

1 km N.E. of the Cattlegrid) and encountered in a number

of rotary and percussion drill holes in the Bellamy Wells
area (OK11), the McLeay area (OK20) and the Gully area
(between the Main Open Cut and the Gunson camp).

In the MG14D core, the rock consists of dark :
mudstone bands interbedded with light grey to yellowish
siltstone and fine sandstone bands (Fig. 7). Mudstone
beds (A9959), consist mainly of fine-grained carbonate
with minor quartz, illite and sulphide. Occasional
transported quartz—illite and dolomitic mud flakes (Fig. 8)
are present. Sandstone beds (A9960, A9961 and A9962) have
detrital grains of carbonate, quartz and quartzite ina
carbonate-—quartz-illite matrix. Detrital carbonate grains
are a mixture of inorganic dolomite and stromatolite
fragments. Small grains of limonite and leucoxene were
seen in some sections.

Interbedded with the mudstone and siltstone are
bands of intraformational breccia (Fig. 9) consisting of
fragments of fine~grained dolomite (usually oriented
parallel to bedding) in a mudstone or sandstone matrix.

Beds are very thin, ranging from about 2 to 50 mm.

Within each bed, a faint lamination may be present, and a
trace of cross lamination is seen in some of the sandy beds.

PALAEOGEOGRAPHIC INTERPRETATION

4.1 Woocalla Sea

After lithification, the Pandurra Formation was
uplifted and exposed to the agencies of erosion forming
a surface of low ridges and broad valleys. Evidence for
this can be seen on present day re-exposed Pandurra
surfaces on the western side of Pernatty Lagoon. This
erosion surface is marked by a quartzite breccia with a
red clay and sand matrix (Fig. 11) which probably
represents a fossil scree or soil horizon.

The main feature of the post-Pandurra palaeo-
surface is a series of hills known as the Pernatty
Culmination, extending northwards from "Yudnapinna”
homestead to the northern end of Lake Windabout. Drilling
and mapping have shown that the main post-Pandurra ridge
on the western side of Pernatty Lagoon extends further
westwards into the Oakden Hills area as a complex of low
ridges and valleys. To the north, west and south of
Oakden Hills, the post-—Pandurra palaeo-surface slopes
away to a broad shallow basin, rising again at Lake
MacFarlane in the west and in the vicinity of Strawbridge
Hill to the south. ‘

The "McLeay Beds" were deposited as a thin veneer
on the post-Pandurra palaeo-surface in scattered localities,
but have little effect on the post-Pandurra topography.

During Woocalla time, the post-Pandurra erosion
surface was largely inundated by a marine transgression,
to form a shallow sea, partly enclosed by land. The various
Pandurra highs represent islands in this sea -~ at Strawbridge
Hill, Mt. Mosely, Lake Windabout and the main post-—Pandurra
ridge stretching along the western edge of Pernatty Lagoon
(Fig. 12). To the east of the Pernatty Culmination, the
Woocalla Sea apparently extended to Lake Torrens, but there
is no data available for this area.

4.2 Theoretical Pattern of Carbonate Deposition

"The general trend of increasing depth away from
shoreline in most shallow seas affects waves and currents
in a predictable fashion. In turn, differential action
of waves and currents on the bottom affects grain size
and sorting of the sediment, and causes formation of
various sedimentary structures as well as influencing
certain factors that control biotic distribution. Asa
result, a distinct and rather consistent pattern of
sedimentary facies grading laterally into one another
perpendicular to the shore is established." (Heckel, 1972).

Irwin (1966) described the theoretical model for
non-detrital sedimentation in an epicontinental sea. He
recognised three major zones characterised by differnt
intensities of water agitation and resulting from the effect
of different relative depths on the position at which waves
and currents impinge on the sea bottom and dissipate their
energy. Heckel (1972) compared Irwin's model to the actual
facies changes in the sediments of the Persian Gulf (Fig.
13) which is a modern epicontinental sea with extensive
carbonate sediments extending seawards from the Trucial
Coast along the southern shore.

zone I : In deeper water below effective wave base,
the bottom is sufficiently undisturbed to
allow fine sediment to settle out. Sedimenta-
tion may be very slow, for it is dependent
solely on detritus derived from the adjacent
shoreward zone for the source of mud. This
zone of reduced deposition would also be a
site of formation of authigenic minerals such
as glauconite. Thus in the deeper water along
the axis of the Persian Gulf, mud is accumulating.
It is predominantly carbonate with a subordinate
amount of shells and fine terrigenous material.
Organic content of the sediment is high, and the
resulting rock type will be dark shaley, shelly
calcilutite.

zone II : In the next shoreward zone, effective wave base
intersects the bottom surface and sediments are
deposited in water agitated by waves. Little
mud can settle out, so mainly sand-size and
coarser material is deposited. In the absence
of detrital influx, the material is largely
skeletal debris, forming calcarenites. Cross-
bedding and ripplemarks are characteristic.
In the Persian Gulf, the deeper-water muds
grade shoreward into skeletal calcarenite, with
glauconite in the deeper offshore part of the
facies.

Also in this zone reefs and oolite sands develop
under certain conditions. In the Persian Gulf,
along the shallowest fringe of the calcarenite
zone are coral reefs and oolite shoals associated
with tidal deltas and channel complexes.

Zone III +: In the nearshore zone of shallowest water, the
environment is again quiet because waves and
currents are damped as their energy is dissipated
on the shallowing sea bottom. Thus mud is again
allowed to accumulate, its source in part being
shoreward transport of the fine products of
disintegration from the adjacent seaward zone.
Lime mud may also be derived by direct physico-
chemical precipitation from sea water. The
resulting rock type is calcilutite, often with
faecal pellets. If the climate is arid, evapo-
rites may form in this zone. In the Persian

‘cule, behind the reef area are very shallow
lagoons with accumulations of lime mud containing
“a large amount of faecal pellets.

tidal environments. The intertidal zone consists
of extensive flats covered with blue-green algal
mats, as found in the Persian Gulf. The algal
mats trap fine calcareous mud and silt, detrital

which become filled with carbonate mud, sand
and coarser fragments.

The supratidal zone is covered by water only by
spring or storm tides, and lies exposed sub-
aerially for long periods of time. The sediments
of this zone are described in some detail by
Lucia (1972). They consist mainly of laminated
carbonate mud containing fine grained dolomite,
gypsum crystals and less commonly anhydrite and
celestite. Alternating wetting and drying
lithifies and tends to break the supratidal
sediment into lithoclasts. Although they are
indicative of the supratidal environment, such
lithoclasts may be transported and thus deposited
in other environment.

4.4 Reducing Environments

Reducing environments occur where the lack of
atmospheric oxygen allows organic matter to be preserved.
Partial anaerobic decay of accumulated organic debris
produces methane and a number of other largely unknown
organic substances. Sulphur from original protein is
converted to hydrogen sulphide. Some sulphur combines
with iron to form iron sulphides, which eventually
crystallise as pyrite.

In shallow seas, such as the Woocalla Sea, reducing
environments may occur :-

(i) In deeper water, below the zone affected by waves
and currents, i.e. the black shale and dolomitic
shale facies.

(ii) In shallow lagoons protected from wave and current
activity by reefs or sand banks, i.e. the laminated

dolomite facies.

BASE OF WOOCALLA DOLOMITE

5.1 Basal Breccia and Conglomerate

In many places the base of the Woocalla Dolomite
is marked by a breccia or conglomerate consisting of
fragments of dolomite and clay, and fragments or pebbles
of Pandurra quartzite in a carbonate matrix. Ferruginisa-

quartzite matrix. In thin-section, the quartzite matrix
is seen to be silicified dolomite. (Curtis, 1973A).

black shale with lenticles of quartz sandstone (reworked
Pandurra grains) and angular fragments of quartzite in the

In part of the Pandurra area, the Woocalla Dolomite
directly overlies the Gawler Range Volcanics. The basal
Woocalla here is a conglomerate containing pebbles and
cobbles of acid volcanics in a sandstone matrix with a
dolomite cement.

7.3 Comparison with Other Areas

Bituminous shales throughout the world show
abnormally high metal contents. They form in stagnant,
non-oxygenated environments which favour the precipitation
of sulphides. The Woocalla Dolomite has been compared to
the'Kupferschieferof northern Europe by Dunlop and Dunlop
(1970), and to the Zambian Copperbelt by Johns (1972).

In the Copperbelt it is suggested (Brandt, 1962:
Garlick, 1972) that sulphides formed in lagoons in which
reducing conditions prevailed. The lagoons were partly
enclosed by basement highs, sand dunes and algal reefs;
differential compaction of sediments over the basement
highs and the dunes allowed perpetuation of the lagoons.
The ore formations are commonly barren over basement highs.

7.4 Controls for Mineralisation

The following parameters appear to be important
in localising base metal mineralisation in the Woocalla
Dolomite: -—

7.4.1 Facies Changes -— metal sulphides occur in
two distinct facies of the dolomite.

a} Dolomitic mud and silt (Sweet Nell-type),
deposited in very shallow water, probably
in a supra-tidal environment.

b) Dolomitic shale, with Cu mineralisation
closer inshore and Pb-Zn offshore.

In both cases, the actual controlling factor
may be a rapid change from oxidising to reducing
conditions as proposed by Lur'ye and Gablina (1972)
for the Mansfeld-type deposits.

7.4.2 Depressions in the post-Pandurra palaeo-
surface: higher metal concentrations appear to be
associated with local depressions (basins or channels)
in the post-~Pandurra palaeo-surface. In the Winnie
Pinnie-Trevenna area, the highest Pb-Zn concentrations
are aligned with elongated depressions. At Sweet Nell
Mine, the mineralisation occurs in an outlier of shale
which was formerly part of a narrow channel, perhaps

7.5.3 Geophysics

The MG14 area was delineated as a target
by the INPUT survey (Langron, 1973), which also
outlined Anomaly "C" in the Powerline area. Subsequent
drilling near Anomaly "C" (hole PL1) showed that
dolomitic mud fills what appears to be a depression
in the post-Pandurra palaeo-surface. This material
may be mineralised but assays are not yet available.

Follow-up geophysics in. the MG14 area produced
a weak E.M. cross-over and a quite good I.P. anomaly
coinciding with the INPUT anomaly.

7.5.4 Conclusion

The INPUT method is possibly picking up
depressions in the post—Pandurra palaeo-~surface. If
so, this method, accompanied by rotary drilling should
be most useful in delineating those areas of Woocalla
Dolomite which satisfy the requirements of facies type
and relation to depressions in the palaeo-surface.
